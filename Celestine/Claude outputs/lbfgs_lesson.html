L-BFGS, from the gradient up

A five-step lesson. Each tab introduces one idea and the terms that go with it, with an interactive demo you can poke at. Highlighted words are defined where they first appear and collected in the glossary.

1 · The landscape, the gradient, and why gradient descent zig-zags

Everything starts with an objective function f(x): a single number we want to make as small as possible, computed from a vector of parameters x = (x₁, …, xn). Picture f as a landscape over the parameters: the optimiser is a hiker in fog who can only feel the ground under their feet.

In your THz extraction, x is (n, κ, d) for every layer — 6 numbers for the quartz/Si bilayer, 15 for the five-layer stack — and f is the rMSE between the measured and simulated pulse. One evaluation of f is one run of the transfer-matrix model.

The hiker's one instrument is the gradient ∇f: the vector of partial derivatives (∂f/∂x₁, …, ∂f/∂xn). Autograd gives it to you for the price of about one extra model evaluation. Three facts about it matter:

(a) It points in the direction of steepest increase, so −∇f is the locally steepest way down. (b) Its length says how steep the slope is. (c) It is always perpendicular to the contour line through the point.

Gradient descent just walks downhill:

xk+1 = xk − α ∇f(xk)

where α is the step size (machine-learning people say learning rate). Here is the whole problem with it: the gradient knows the direction but says nothing about how far to go, and the right distance is different in different directions. In the bowl below the loss rises 25 times faster along y than along x. A step size small enough to be stable in y (α < 2/25 = 0.08) is a crawl in x. The result is the famous zig-zag: the hiker bounces across the narrow valley while creeping along it.

How bad this is depends on the condition number κ = (steepest curvature)/(flattest curvature). Roughly, gradient descent needs on the order of κ iterations. The bowl has κ = 25.

Your sensitivity analysis of the quartz/Si fit found σ₁/σ₆ ≈ 5500 between the "total optical path" direction and the κ directions. That is why Adam spent 18 000 of 20 000 iterations drifting in κ: it was the zig-zag in six dimensions.

A line search replaces the fixed α by a trial: try a step, and if f did not decrease enough, halve it and try again. Here it helps to name the search direction p: the direction the optimiser has decided to move in, so that every step has the form x + αp. For gradient descent p = −∇f; for Newton and L-BFGS it will be something better. The "enough" test is the Armijo condition

f(x + αp) ≤ f(x) + c · α · (∇f · p),    c ≈ 10⁻⁴

The quantity ∇f·p is the slope of f along p (negative for a downhill direction), so α·∇f·p is the decrease a straight-line model of f would promise for that step; the condition only asks for a small fraction c of it. If the trial α fails the test, α is halved and the test repeated. It removes the instability but not the zig-zag — tick the box in the demo to see it. The direction itself is what needs fixing, and for that we need to know the curvature. That is tab 2.

2 · Newton's method: divide the gradient by the curvature

Start in one dimension. Near a point x the function looks like a parabola, its second-order Taylor model:

f(x + p) ≈ f(x) + f′(x)·p + ½ f″(x)·p²

f′ is the slope (the gradient), f″ is the curvature: how fast the slope itself changes. Where is the bottom of that parabola? Set its derivative to zero: f′ + f″ p = 0, so

pNewton = − f′(x) / f″(x)

That is the whole idea: gradient divided by curvature. Steep slope but very curved (a narrow valley) → small step. Gentle slope and almost flat → large step. Note that there is no α any more — the curvature fixes the scale of the step, and on an exact parabola one step lands on the minimum.

In n dimensions the curvature is not one number but a matrix, the Hessian H, with entries Hij = ∂²f/∂xi∂xj. It is symmetric and n×n. The Taylor model and its minimiser become

f(x + p) ≈ f + ∇fᵀp + ½ pᵀHp   ⟹   pNewton = − H−1 ∇f

"Dividing by the curvature" is now "multiplying by the inverse Hessian". Geometrically, H−1 is a stretch: it shrinks the gradient along directions where the landscape is steeply curved and stretches it along flat directions, so the step points at the bottom of the valley instead of across it. The ellipse in the demo's inset is exactly that stretch, drawn as what H−1 does to a unit circle. On the bowl, Newton's step is one jump to the minimum from anywhere, whatever the condition number.

So why not always use Newton?

Three reasons, and quasi-Newton methods are the answer to all three.

Cost. The Hessian has n(n+1)/2 second derivatives, and solving H p = −∇f costs about n³ operations. For your 15 parameters that is cheap; for a 10⁶-parameter network it is impossible. More to the point, we only ever use H−1 multiplied by a vector — we never need the matrix itself.

Validity. The step goes to the stationary point of the parabola, which is a minimum only if the curvature is positive in every direction — the Hessian must be positive definite (pᵀH p > 0 for every p, i.e. bowl-shaped all round). Far from the minimum it often is not. Slide the 1-D demo into the hump between the two wells and watch Newton head uphill to the top.

Trust. The parabola is only a local model; a full Newton step far away can overshoot. Practical methods keep a line search as a safety net and try α = 1 first, which is accepted almost always near the solution.

In the loss-landscape notes you found that the autograd Hessian of the MAE-dominated loss gave a spurious negative eigenvalue — the "validity" problem in the wild. The sensitivity matrix JᵀJ you used instead is the Gauss–Newton approximation of the Hessian, which is always positive semi-definite.

3 · Quasi-Newton: measure the curvature with the steps you take anyway

Back to one dimension. Suppose you cannot compute f″, but you have just taken a step from x₀ to x₁ and you evaluated the gradient at both ends. You moved by

s = x₁ − x₀   (the step)      y = f′(x₁) − f′(x₀)   (the change in gradient)

Curvature is "how fast the slope changes", so f″ ≈ y / s. This is the secant (finite-difference) estimate, and it costs nothing: both gradients were computed anyway. The inverse curvature is s / y, so a Newton-like step from x₁ is

x₂ = x₁ − (s / y) · f′(x₁)

Play with the first demo: the secant estimate is usually within a few percent of the true f″ once the two points are close, and the secant step lands nearly where Newton would.

Several dimensions: one pair, one direction

Keep the same two measurements, now as vectors: the curvature pair

sk = xk+1 − xk      yk = ∇f(xk+1) − ∇f(xk)

For a quadratic landscape the Hessian relates them exactly: H s = y. So whatever matrix we use as our estimate of the inverse Hessian — call it Hk (from here on H without a subscript means the true Hessian, Hk our estimate of its inverse) — we want it to satisfy the secant equation

Hk+1 yk = sk

Read it as: "if the gradient changes by y, the position must have changed by s." The catch is that this is n equations for n(n+1)/2 unknowns. One pair tells you the curvature along the direction you walked and nothing about the others. Quasi-Newton methods therefore accumulate: every step contributes one pair, and after a handful of steps in different directions the estimate is good everywhere that matters.

The BFGS update

BFGS (Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno, 1970) is the rule for folding a new pair into the current estimate. Of all matrices that satisfy the secant equation and stay symmetric, it picks the one closest to the old Hk — change as little as possible, learn only what the new pair says:

Hk+1 = (I − ρ s yᵀ) Hk (I − ρ y sᵀ) + ρ s sᵀ,     ρ = 1 / (yᵀs)

Each piece has a job. The scalar ρ = 1/(s·y) is the normalisation that makes the 1-D secant identity work in vector form. The factor (I − ρ y sᵀ) acting on a vector v gives v − y (s·v)/(s·y): it removes from v the part that lies along the direction just probed — "forget what the old estimate believed about this direction". The sandwich with the transposed factor on the other side keeps the result symmetric. Finally ρ s sᵀ inserts the freshly measured inverse curvature: applied to y it returns s (s·y)/(s·y) = s, exactly the secant equation. You can check that the first term kills y outright, since (I − ρ y sᵀ)y = y − y = 0, so Hk+1y = s as required.

Two more facts. The update changes Hk only in the plane spanned by s and Hky — it is a rank-two update. And Hk+1 stays positive definite (so −Hk+1∇f is still a downhill direction) if and only if the curvature condition s·y > 0 holds: the measured curvature along the step must be positive. A Wolfe line search (Armijo plus a check that the slope along p has become less negative) guarantees this. Codes that skip it must throw the pair away when s·y ≤ 0, or the estimate turns into nonsense.

This is the NaN you saw with "plain full-band L-BFGS … unbounded line search" in the five-layer diagnosis: a step that walks through a region of negative curvature, a pair with s·y ≤ 0 that is not rejected, and the direction stops being a descent direction. The strong-Wolfe search used in the limits study is the fix.

4 · L-BFGS: the same idea without ever building the matrix

BFGS stores an n×n matrix and does n² work per step. L-BFGS (limited-memory BFGS, Nocedal 1980) notices two things. First, Hk is nothing but the starting guess H₀ with k rank-two updates stacked on top, and each update is fully described by its pair (si, yi). Second, we only ever need the product Hk∇f, never Hk itself. So: keep only the last m pairs (the memory, typically m = 5–20; SciPy's default is 10), and compute Hk∇f by applying those m updates one after another to the vector ∇f. Storage 2mn numbers, work about 4mn per step.

Throwing away old pairs is also a feature. On a landscape that is not a quadratic, the curvature measured ten steps ago describes a different place; a short memory keeps the estimate local.

The starting guess, γ

Without any pairs the best we can do is a scalar: H₀ = γ I, the same inverse curvature in every direction. L-BFGS sets it from the newest pair,

γk = (s·y) / (y·y)

which is the 1-D secant estimate s/y of tab 3 projected onto the direction of y. Its only job is to get the scale of the step right, so that the trial step α = 1 is usually accepted by the line search. The directional information comes from the pairs.

The two-loop recursion, line by line

Write Vi = I − ρi yisiᵀ. Stacking m BFGS updates on γI gives

Hk = Vᵀk−1···Vᵀk−m (γI) Vk−m···Vk−1 + ρk−1sk−1sᵀk−1 + Vᵀk−1 ρk−2sk−2sᵀk−2 Vk−1 + ···

Ugly, but every factor is "remove the component along one si" or "insert the measured curvature along one si", and applying them to a vector in the right order is cheap. That ordered application is the two-loop recursion:

①  q ← ∇f(xk)  — the vector we are going to transform into the step. ②  First loop, newest pair → oldest. For each stored pair i:  αi ← ρi (si·q)   then   q ← q − αi yi αi is "how much of q lies along si", measured in units of si·yi. Subtracting αiyi is the factor Vi: it strips out the component of the gradient that pair i can account for. The αi are kept for the second loop. ③  Scale.  r ← γ q  — apply the isotropic guess H₀ to whatever is left of the gradient. ④  Second loop, oldest pair → newest. For each i:  βi ← ρi (yi·r)   then   r ← r + (αi − βi) si This is Viᵀ followed by the ρisisiᵀ term, merged into one line: it puts back, along si, the right amount of step for the gradient component that was removed in loop 1 — the measured inverse curvature along that direction. ⑤  p ← −r.  Now r = Hk∇f exactly, with the matrix never formed. Line-search along p starting at α = 1, move, store the new pair, drop the oldest if there are more than m.

A way to feel what it computes: in the directions spanned by the stored si the step is Newton-like, using the measured curvatures; in every other direction it is γ times steepest descent. With m ≥ n independent pairs on a quadratic, the recursion reproduces the exact H−1, which is why in the 2-D playground the blue ellipse snaps onto the green one after two or three steps.

For your 15-parameter stack with m = 10 the recursion costs 40·15 flops per iteration — nothing next to the transfer-matrix evaluation. The coarse-to-fine trick that gave 12/12 convergence works with the memory: the smoothed loss has a gentler, more quadratic valley, so the pairs collected there describe the curvature well, and the memory is refreshed as the band opens up.

What is going on

Glossary

objective / loss f(x)
The number being minimised, a function of the parameter vector x. In the THz fits: rMSE between measured and simulated pulses.
gradient ∇f
Vector of first partial derivatives. Points uphill, perpendicular to contours, length = steepness. Autograd supplies it.
Hessian H
Symmetric n×n matrix of second derivatives Hij = ∂²f/∂xi∂xj: the curvature in every direction. Its eigenvectors are the principal axes of the local bowl, its eigenvalues the curvatures along them.
positive definite
pᵀHp > 0 for every p ≠ 0: curvature positive in all directions, so the quadratic model has a unique minimum. Required for −H⁻¹∇f to be a descent direction.
condition number κ
λmax/λmin of the Hessian. Gradient descent needs ~κ iterations; Newton-type methods are insensitive to it.
step size α / learning rate
Scalar multiplying the search direction. Fixed in plain gradient descent; chosen by a line search in Newton-type methods (α = 1 tried first).
search direction p
The direction of the next move; every iteration is xk+1 = xk + α pk. Gradient descent: p = −∇f. Newton: p = −H⁻¹∇f. BFGS / L-BFGS: p = −Hk∇f with the estimated Hk. A descent direction is one with ∇f·p < 0 (points downhill).
line search
Procedure that picks α along a given direction p. Backtracking: start at α = 1, halve until the Armijo condition holds. Wolfe: Armijo plus a curvature condition on the new slope, which guarantees s·y > 0.
Armijo condition
f(x+αp) ≤ f(x) + c₁ α ∇f·p with c₁ ≈ 10⁻⁴: the decrease must be at least a small fraction of what the linear model promised.
Newton step
p = −H⁻¹∇f: minimiser of the local quadratic model. Exact in one step on a quadratic.
secant equation
Hk+1 yk = sk: the inverse-Hessian estimate must map the observed gradient change back to the observed step. The n-D version of f″ ≈ y/s.
curvature pair (sk, yk)
sk = xk+1 − xk (the step), yk = ∇fk+1 − ∇fk (the gradient change). One pair = one directional curvature measurement.
curvature condition s·y > 0
Measured curvature along the step is positive. Keeps the BFGS estimate positive definite. Pairs violating it must be rejected (or prevented by a Wolfe line search).
ρk
1/(sk·yk). The normalisation that makes the 1-D identity "inverse curvature = s/y" work for vectors.
BFGS update
Hk+1 = (I − ρ s yᵀ) Hk (I − ρ y sᵀ) + ρ s sᵀ. Rank-two change: remove what Hk believed along s, insert the measured value.
quasi-Newton
Any method that uses a Newton-like step with a curvature estimate built from gradient differences instead of true second derivatives (BFGS, DFP, SR1, L-BFGS).
memory m
Number of recent pairs L-BFGS keeps. Typical 5–20; SciPy maxcor = 10 by default.
γk
(s·y)/(y·y) of the newest pair; the scalar starting guess H₀ = γI. Sets the step scale, not the direction.
two-loop recursion
Algorithm that computes Hk∇f from the m pairs in O(mn): first loop strips gradient components along each si (αi), scaling by γ, second loop adds back the measured steps (αi − βi) si.
L-BFGS-B
L-BFGS with simple bound constraints (SciPy's default for bounded problems). Same recursion, plus a projection onto the box.
Gauss–Newton
For least-squares losses: approximate H ≈ JᵀJ with J the Jacobian of residuals. Always positive semi-definite; this is the "sensitivity matrix" of the loss-landscape notes.
