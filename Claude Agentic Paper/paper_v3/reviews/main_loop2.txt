Abstract: Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy can return the thickness and complex refractive
index of each layer of a stratified sample from one transmitted pulse, but the inverse problem is
unstable for thin or multiple layers. We fit a differentiable transfer-matrix model to the measured
pulse with Adam and L-BFGS and ask what the data determine. Simulations with a measured
reference pulse show noise-limited recovery of a thin film down to a round trip comparable
with the pulse duration. A polyimide film on silicon is recovered only once a reference time
offset is fitted: absolute thicknesses rest on time-zero reproducibility. On a quartz-on-silicon
bilayer the pulse timings fix each layer’s optical thickness and the total thickness to better than
0.1 %. The soft directions left are a transfer of thickness between the layers and the split of the
total attenuation between them. The latter makes the sign of the silicon extinction coefficient
estimator-dependent; a softplus constraint, not a richer model, resolves it. L-BFGS completes
the well-conditioned descent without step-size tuning but stalls at a kink of the mean-absolute
loss. A six-knot dispersive model of a quartz–air–silicon stack determines the air gap an order of
magnitude better than either plate.

1.   Introduction
In terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) a picosecond pulse is recorded as a time-
resolved field through air (the reference) and through the sample; for a thick slab the complex
index follows stably, frequency by frequency [1–4]. For thin or multiple layers the delayed
replicas of the pulse from internal reflections (Fabry–Pérot echoes) overlap and the problem
becomes non-convex [5], precisely where thickness gauging is in demand [6]; fitting the whole
trace [7] does not remove this.
   We treat the extraction as an inverse problem [8], fitting a differentiable transfer-matrix method
(TMM) [9, 10] for thickness 𝑑, index 𝑛 and extinction coefficient 𝜅 of every layer with Adam [11]
or L-BFGS [12]. We give the thin-film limit, the loss landscape of a quartz-on-silicon fit
(sensitivities, Cramér–Rao bounds, optimiser paths [13]), Adam versus L-BFGS, and a six-knot
dispersive model.

4.     Simulated extractions and a thin film
A noise-free five-layer stack (fifteen parameters) is recovered to 0.002 % in 𝑛, 𝑑 and 0.3 % in 𝜅.
For a thin film on a thick substrate we sweep the film thickness with the reference of Section 5 and
white noise (Fig. 1(a)). The scatter equals the Cramér–Rao bound (Section 6.2) to within 25 % in
𝑛 and 𝑑, without bias: the sweep is noise-limited. The median error in 𝑑 exceeds 1 % between 7
and 10 µm, where the round trip falls below two thirds of the reference FWHM (0.36 ps) and the
film echo merges with the main pulse.

on silicon [sample details; float-zone Si?] (Table 1). On a common time axis the fit returns an
impossible 𝑛Si = 3.26 [14], which absorbs a timing error (every start conserves the pulse delay
and Si echo spacing), likely a time-zero drift between scans 23 min apart; 10 fs is 3 µm of total
thickness. With a fitted time offset 𝜏 and amplitude 𝑎, 𝑦 = 𝑎 F −1 [𝑇 F [𝑥] 𝑒 −𝑖 𝜔 𝜏 ], the misfit falls

also with 𝑛Si fixed. The best starts agree on 𝑛PI 𝑑PI to 0.2 %; the attenuation moves from substrate
to film, so its split is undetermined. A PI-like simulation puts the 1 % limit in 𝑑 at 10–15 µm, so

5.     Experimental extraction: quartz on silicon
A quartz plate (q) and silicon wafer (Si), clamped in contact without adhesive [nominal and
mechanical thicknesses, supplier], were measured under nitrogen (Fig. 2) and fitted with Adam
(𝜂 = 10−4 ) from three starts, which follow the same valley (Table 2) to a budget iterate on a
plateau (6 × 104 iterations change the loss by < 0.02 %). The quartz index is that of fused
silica [1, 3] [confirm sample material]. 𝑛Si is 0.9 % above the literature value [14] and 𝜅Si is
negative. The residual RMS is 41𝜎: the model, not the noise, limits the fit.

6.2.               Cramér–Rao bounds, and why the sign of 𝜅 Si is a model artefact
(i) For an exact model and white noise 𝜎, any unbiased estimator obeys the Cramér–Rao (CR)
bound cov( 𝑥) ˆ ≥ 𝜎 2 (𝐽 ⊤ 𝐽) −1 [15]; (ii) 𝐽 is taken at the Adam point; (iii) the bounds are in Table 2.
(iv) 𝜅Si is 77 bounds from zero, 𝑛Si about a hundred from the literature value; but the residual
RMS (41𝜎) exceeds any defensible noise estimate, so noise can neither explain the sign nor make
it significant. With the residual in place of 𝜎, or a sandwich covariance, the ratio is below 2 at
the Adam point and 4–10 at the least-squares point. (v) The data fix the total attenuation; its
split depends on how the estimator weights the misfit (𝜅Si from −0.007 to +0.004, Table 2). (vi)
Richer models keep the sign: six knots more than halve the misfit but leave 𝜅Si < 0 across the
band, an interface gap or adhesive gains little, and a free 𝜏 gives 𝜅 Si > 0 only with an impossible
𝑛Si = 3.64. The sign, like 𝑛Si , is a model artefact: it lies along a direction the data do not
determine and is set by the model error.
   The remedy is a constraint, 𝜅 𝑗 = 𝜅 (0)    𝑗 softplus(𝛽𝜃 𝑗 )/softplus(𝛽), 𝛽 = 5, with the smooth
positive softplus(𝑧) = ln(1 + 𝑒 𝑧 ), which keeps a gradient where a rectifier would freeze at zero.
It drives 𝜅 Si to zero, as for high-resistivity silicon [14], at 0.3 % in loss, moving 𝑛, 𝑑 along the
thickness-transfer valley.
7.                L-BFGS versus Adam
L-BFGS (memory 20, up to three restarts on a stall) is run from the same starts, parameters and
loss (Fig. 4); one Adam iteration and one L-BFGS evaluation each cost a forward and a backward
pass. L-BFGS crosses the Phase-I bowl in about thirty iterations and reaches its final level in
123–154 evaluations (250–299 with the confirming restarts); Adam needs 5000–5900 iterations
at 𝜂 = 10−4 and 360–460 at 3 × 10−3 , none diverging. The advantage of L-BFGS is not a fixed
speed-up but that no step size needs tuning.
   L-BFGS then stops 3.7 % above Adam’s end point at a kink, not a barrier: along −∇L the loss
rises at once (one-sided slope +0.023, gradient prediction −0.036) as two residuals change sign.
Yet it falls monotonically towards Adam’s end point: the known failure of BFGS on non-smooth
functions [16]. With the smooth rMSE it converges (gradient down six decades, starts agreeing
to 0.2 nm) to another point of the thickness-transfer valley, no closer to the physical silicon
constants. For white noise and the full band a combined time- and frequency-domain loss adds
no information (same CR bounds).

9.      Discussion and conclusions
A differentiable TMM measures what pulse timings measure: each layer’s optical thickness and,
given time zero, the total thickness. The same plates measured twice (Sections 5, 8) differ in
quartz 𝑛 𝑑 by over a percent, an accuracy of ∼1 % against a precision of 0.01 %, likely set by time
zero. L-BFGS needs no step size and suits a smooth loss. With residuals far above the noise, the
realistic uncertainty is the estimator spread (2 % in 𝑑q , 6 % in 𝑑Si ); a weak absorption’s sign is
best fixed by a constraint. [mechanically measured thicknesses to be included]
