Abstract: Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy can in principle return the thickness and
complex refractive index of every layer of a stratified sample from one transmission trace, but the
inverse problem is unstable once the sample is thin or has several layers. We fit a differentiable
transfer-matrix model to the measured pulse with the gradient optimisers Adam and L-BFGS
and extract 𝑛, 𝜅 and 𝑑 of every layer. Simulations with a measured reference pulse locate the
film thickness below which a thin film can no longer be separated from its substrate, and a
polyimide film on silicon at that threshold is recovered in agreement with profilometry. On a
quartz-on-silicon bilayer the sensitivity matrix of the forward model constrains the total optical
path three orders of magnitude better than the extinction coefficients and separates 𝑛 from 𝑑 only
through the Fabry–Pérot echoes; the optimiser trajectory, projected onto the principal planes of
its own path, shows Adam creeping along a flat 𝜅 valley for most of its budget, whereas L-BFGS
completes the well-conditioned part of the descent in a few hundred evaluations. Cramér–Rao
bounds show that the negative silicon 𝜅 of the frequency-independent fit is a model artefact,
removed by a six-knot dispersive model of a quartz–air–silicon stack.

1.   Introduction

Terahertz time-domain spectroscopy (THz-TDS) yields the complex refractive index of a sample
from the transmitted field of a picosecond pulse [1–3]. For an optically thick slab the classical
frequency-by-frequency inversion [2] is stable and noise-limited [4]; for thin samples or several
layers internal reflections overlap, the transfer function admits several solutions and the objective
becomes non-convex [5], so thickness and index can no longer be separated, precisely in the
thickness-gauging regime where THz-TDS is most in demand [6]. Fitting the whole time trace [7]
replaces the inversion by a non-convex optimisation whose behaviour is rarely examined.
   Here the extraction is treated as an inverse problem [8] with the transfer-matrix method
(TMM) [9] as forward model, implemented with automatic differentiation [10] so that 𝑑, 𝑛 and 𝜅
of every layer are fitted simultaneously with Adam [11] or L-BFGS [12]. Simulations with a
measured reference pulse locate where a thin film on a substrate ceases to be extractable, and
a polyimide film on silicon confirms the prediction. The loss landscape of an experimental
quartz-on-silicon fit is then analysed through the sensitivity matrix of the forward model and,
borrowing from deep learning [13], through projections of the optimiser trajectory onto the
planes in which it actually moves; a six-knot dispersive model of a quartz–air–silicon stack closes
the paper.

5.     Experimental extraction: quartz on silicon
A quartz plate bonded to a silicon wafer [bonding method, nominal thicknesses and supplier to
be added] was measured with a Menlo Systems TeraSmart spectrometer under nitrogen purge
(1000 waveforms over 15 min, 2425 points at 33 fs; 𝜎 = 2.9 × 10−4 , peak-to-𝜎 ratio 7.9 × 103 )
and fitted with Eq. (4) and Adam at 𝜂 = 10−4 for 2 × 104 iterations from three starts bracketing
the expected quartz index (Fig. 2, Table 2). The starts converge to the same point to four figures
in 𝑛 and 𝑑; the quartz index is that of fused silica [1, 3] rather than crystalline quartz [confirm
sample material], the silicon index is within a percent of the literature value [14], and the silicon
extinction coefficient is negative, which is unphysical. The loss falls by an order of magnitude in
the first 8 % of the iterations and only slowly thereafter, and the mean absolute residual at the
optimum is 15𝜎 (Fig. 2(b)).

Whether the negative silicon extinction coefficient is a statistical fluctuation or a systematic
effect the sensitivity matrix decides, step by step. (i) If the model were exact and the residual
white noise of standard deviation 𝜎, the covariance of any unbiased estimator would obey
the Cramér–Rao bound cov( 𝑥)     ˆ ≥ 𝜎 2 (𝐽 ⊤ 𝐽) −1 [15], a property of the measurement, not of the
optimiser; (ii) 𝐽 is Eq. (6) at the optimum. (iii) The square roots of the diagonal are the bounds
in Table 2: the indices at the 10−4 level, the thicknesses at 31 nm, 𝜅 2 at 1.9 × 10−5 . (iv) The
fitted 𝜅 2 = −1.5 × 10−3 is therefore 77𝜎 from zero: noise cannot have produced the sign. (v)

buys almost nothing. (vi) The sign is therefore the frequency-independent model absorbing the
high-frequency amplitude excess of Fig. 2(c) through the one parameter the data leave nearly

7.   L-BFGS versus Adam
Adam creeps along the 𝜅 valley at a fixed normalised speed, whereas a quasi-Newton method
learns the curvature. We therefore repeated the fit with L-BFGS (strong-Wolfe line search, history
20) from the same three starts with the same scaled parameters and loss (Fig. 4). L-BFGS
cuts across the Phase-I bowl instead of tracing Adam’s L-shaped route and reaches, in 250–300
evaluations, the loss that Adam reaches after 5000–5900 iterations; then it stops. The stop
is a kink, not a barrier: the line search finds no decrease even for steps of 10−8 , because the
mean-absolute term of Eq. (4) is not differentiable where residual samples change sign, yet the
loss decreases monotonically along the straight line to Adam’s end point, 3.7 % lower. The two
end points differ by 5.7 µm in 𝑑1 and 𝑑2 , with 𝜅 Si still positive at the kink, while 𝑛1 𝑑1 and 𝑛2 𝑑2
agree to 0.012 % and 0.034 %: what L-BFGS reaches twenty times cheaper is the part of the
fit the data constrain; what it misses is the flat 𝜅 valley of Section 6, where the end point is
decided by how the loss weights the model error. A smooth loss is the right companion for a
quasi-Newton method: with the rMSE, or the log-cosh term alone, L-BFGS converges without a
stall to yet another point of the 𝑛 𝑑 valley. A combined time- and frequency-domain loss adds no
information, its Cramér–Rao bounds being those of the time trace.

in loss).

9.   Discussion and conclusions
A differentiable TMM fitted by gradient optimisers degrades gracefully in 𝑛 and 𝑑 and erratically
in 𝜅 as a film thins, failing once the film echo no longer leaves the main pulse; on the quartz/Si
bilayer it converges reproducibly but slowly. All of this follows from the sensitivity spectrum of
the measurement: the total optical path is constrained thousands of times better than the extinction
coefficients, the per-layer 𝑛–𝑑 split only by the echoes. L-BFGS reaches the data-constrained part
of the optimum twenty times cheaper than Adam and, with a smooth loss, is the better default.
Uncertainties from (𝐽 ⊤ 𝐽) −1 [4, 8, 15] must be read alongside the ratio of residual to noise floor:
when it is far above unity, the honest uncertainty on 𝑑 is the spread between fits that weight the
model error differently, here 2 %. [mechanically measured thickness to be included?]
